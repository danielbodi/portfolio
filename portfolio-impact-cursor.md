# Portfolio Impact Rewrite — Cursor Instructions

## Purpose

Help me update my portfolio so it communicates **my real impact, ownership, decisions, influence, and outcomes** — not just what I designed or built.

The portfolio should make it easier for a hiring manager to understand:

- what problems I was responsible for,
- what decisions I personally made,
- what I changed,
- how I influenced product/design/dev teams,
- what constraints I had,
- what improved because of my work,
- and where my impact was limited by the organization rather than by my own approach.

The target positioning is around **Senior / Lead Design Systems / Design Engineer / UX Engineer** roles, with enough evidence of strategic influence when it genuinely exists.

---

# Critical rule: do not invent anything

**Never fabricate, assume, extrapolate, or beautify facts.**

Do not invent:

- metrics,
- percentages,
- adoption numbers,
- team sizes,
- delivery speed improvements,
- cost savings,
- business impact,
- user feedback,
- decisions I did not make,
- ownership I did not have,
- technologies I did not use,
- processes I did not lead.

If a stronger case study needs information that is missing, **ask me for it before writing the claim**.

Use placeholders such as:

> [NEED INFO: How many teams/apps used this?]

or ask me directly in chat.

If something sounds plausible but is not proven by the repository or by my answers, treat it as unknown.

---

# Should we use STAR?

Yes — but **do not make the portfolio read like an interview answer**.

Use STAR as an internal structure:

- **S — Situation:** What was happening? What was broken, unclear, slow, inconsistent, or risky?
- **T — Task / Responsibility:** What was I actually responsible for?
- **A — Actions:** What decisions did I make? What did I personally do? Who did I influence?
- **R — Results:** What changed as a result?

For portfolio case studies, extend it into:

## STAR + Impact + Constraints

1. **Context / Situation**
2. **Problem**
3. **My role and scope**
4. **Constraints**
5. **Key decisions**
6. **Actions**
7. **Collaboration / influence**
8. **Result**
9. **Impact / evidence**
10. **What I learned / what I would improve**

Do not necessarily use these exact headings on the website. They are a reasoning framework.

The final content should feel like a strong case study, not a STAR worksheet.

---

# Core transformation

The current portfolio should move away from:

> “I created a component library, designed screens, documented patterns, and worked with developers.”

toward:

> “Here was the problem. Here is what I owned. Here are the decisions I made. Here is what changed because of those decisions.”

For every section, ask:

> **Could another designer on the same project claim exactly the same sentence?**

If yes, the sentence is probably too generic.

Rewrite toward my specific contribution.

# Portfolio narrative principles

## 1. Separate contribution from context

Make a clear distinction between:

- what already existed,
- what the organization decided,
- what the team did collectively,
- what I proposed,
- what I personally decided,
- what I influenced,
- what I implemented,
- what changed because of me.

Avoid language that accidentally makes me look like I owned decisions I did not own.

Also avoid language that hides genuine ownership behind vague “we” statements.

Use **“I”** when the action or decision was mine.

Use **“we”** when it was genuinely shared.

---

## 2. Show decisions, not only deliverables

Do not stop at:

- built a component,
- created a Figma library,
- wrote documentation,
- created prototypes,
- ran workshops,
- implemented Storybook.

Try to uncover:

- Why was this needed?
- What alternatives existed?
- Why did I choose this approach?
- What trade-off did I make?
- Who disagreed?
- What did I have to convince people of?
- What did I intentionally *not* do?
- What changed after the decision?

---

## 3. Show influence

For senior/lead/staff-leaning roles, identify evidence of influence such as:

- aligning design and engineering,
- changing a team's approach,
- introducing shared standards,
- creating governance,
- creating a contribution model,
- coaching developers/designers,
- resolving cross-team inconsistency,
- influencing roadmap or architecture,
- getting teams to adopt shared components,
- pushing back on weak decisions,
- improving how decisions were made.

Only include these when supported by evidence.

---

## 4. Show constraints honestly

Constraints are useful when they explain why the work was difficult.

Examples:

- consultant vs internal employee,
- existing organizational decisions,
- legacy architecture,
- limited design maturity,
- multiple teams,
- technical debt,
- partial ownership,
- limited access to users,
- roadmap already committed,
- dependency on other teams.

Do **not** use constraints as excuses.

Instead, explain:

> “Given these constraints, this is what I was still able to change.”

## Solidaris

The case study should be especially clear about **organizational constraints and actual influence**.

Important context to represent accurately:

- I joined an existing organization.
- Important decisions may already have been made before I arrived.
- My position as a consultant affected how much authority/influence I initially had.
- Part of the challenge was earning a place in the team and increasing my ability to influence decisions.

Do not turn this into a negative story.

The useful narrative is:

> “I entered an environment with existing decisions and limited initial influence. Here is how I identified where I could have impact, built credibility, and progressively changed/improved X.”

### Ask me for details such as:

- Which decisions were already fixed before I arrived?
- What did I disagree with?
- What did I manage to change anyway?
- What did I propose that was accepted?
- What did I propose that was rejected?
- How long did it take before I gained influence?
- Who did I need to convince?
- What changed in the Design System because of my involvement?
- What changed in the product UX?
- What governance/documentation/adoption work was mine?
- What research activities did I personally initiate?
- What evidence exists of adoption or improved consistency?
- What remains constrained by the organization today?

This case study should make it easy for a hiring manager to distinguish:

**my operating pace**  
from  
**the organization's decision-making pace**.

# Case study structure

For each major project, try to produce a narrative close to this:

## 1. One-line impact statement

A concise statement answering:

> What was the most important thing I changed?

Do not write it until the evidence exists.

---

## 2. Context

Explain the product, company, users, and starting point in a few sentences.

Keep it short.

---

## 3. The problem

Describe the actual problem.

Prefer specific problems over vague statements like:

> “The UX needed improvement.”

Better:

> “Three teams were solving the same interaction differently, making the product inconsistent and forcing developers to maintain parallel implementations.”

Only use specific claims when verified.

---

## 4. My role

Be explicit.

Examples of useful dimensions:

- ownership,
- decision authority,
- responsibilities,
- team position,
- design vs engineering contribution,
- where my influence stopped.

---

## 5. Constraints

Only include constraints that materially affected the work.

---

## 6. Key decisions

This is one of the most important sections.

For each meaningful decision:

### Decision
What did I decide or propose?

### Why
Why was that preferable?

### Alternative
What other option existed?

### Trade-off
What did we gain or sacrifice?

### Outcome
What happened after?

---

## 7. What I did

Prioritize actions with leverage.

Avoid giant feature inventories.

Group low-level work under higher-level decisions.

---

## 8. How I influenced the organization

Where relevant, explain:

- who I aligned,
- who I convinced,
- what changed in the way teams worked,
- what standards became shared,
- what became easier for others.

---

## 9. Outcomes

Separate outcomes into categories.

### User outcome
What became better for users?

### Product outcome
What became better for the product?

### Team outcome
What became easier/faster/clearer for the team?

### System outcome
What became more reusable, scalable, governed, or consistent?

### Business outcome
Only include if there is actual evidence.

---

## 10. Evidence

Use evidence instead of adjectives.

Possible evidence:

- adoption,
- number of products,
- number of teams,
- before/after screenshots,
- reduced variants,
- reduced duplicate components,
- research findings,
- usability test results,
- qualitative developer feedback,
- documented reuse,
- time saved,
- defects reduced,
- contribution frequency,
- migration progress.

Do not invent missing metrics.

If there are no quantitative metrics, qualitative evidence is valid.

---

## 11. Reflection

A short senior-level reflection:

- what I learned,
- what I would change today,
- where the organization constrained the outcome,
- what I would do differently with more authority,
- how the experience changed my approach.

Avoid fake humility.

---

# Questions Cursor should ask me

Whenever information is missing, ask focused questions.

Do not ask 30 questions at once.

Ask **3–6 high-value questions per round**.

Prioritize questions that can materially improve the case study.

## Good questions

- What was the situation before you intervened?
- Which decision was explicitly yours?
- Who did you need to convince?
- What resistance did you face?
- What changed after your intervention?
- How do you know it improved?
- How many teams/products adopted it?
- What would probably have happened if you had not been there?
- Which part are you most proud of?
- Which constraint prevented you from going further?

## Bad questions

- “Can you tell me more?”
- “Was the project successful?”
- “Did users like it?”
- “Did you improve efficiency?”

Be specific.

---

# Handling metrics

Metrics are useful but **not mandatory**.

Never manufacture numbers to make the portfolio stronger.

If no exact metric exists, use honest alternatives:

Instead of:

> “Reduced development time by 40%.”

Use, if true:

> “Developers could reuse an existing implementation instead of rebuilding the pattern separately in each product.”

Instead of:

> “Improved adoption by 60%.”

Use, if true:

> “The same components became shared across multiple products and teams.”

Whenever a number would strengthen a statement, ask me whether I have a reliable number.

---

# Writing style

The portfolio should sound:

- senior,
- confident,
- concrete,
- concise,
- technically credible,
- product-minded,
- non-corporate,
- evidence-based.

Avoid:

- buzzword soup,
- exaggerated leadership language,
- “world-class”,
- “revolutionized”,
- “single-handedly”,
- vague “collaborated with stakeholders” statements,
- generic UX process descriptions,
- long explanations of Double Diamond unless it materially changed the outcome.

Prefer active language.

Bad:

> “A Design System was created to improve consistency.”

Better:

> “I introduced a shared component model so product teams could stop solving the same UI patterns independently.”

Only use the better version if that is factually true.

When reviewing existing portfolio pages, recommend visuals that prove the story.

Useful visuals include:

- before / after,
- architecture diagrams,
- component genealogy,
- design-token structure,
- contribution workflow,
- governance model,
- adoption map,
- product screenshots showing reuse,
- Figma ↔ code mapping,
- decision trees,
- research evidence,
- evolution over time.

Every visual should answer a question.

Avoid decorative mockups that do not prove impact.

# Positioning

The portfolio should make this profile understandable quickly:

> A senior design systems / design engineering profile who can work across product design and frontend implementation, create scalable systems, and help teams adopt them.

However, do not force every case study into this positioning if it does not fit.

The evidence should drive the positioning, not the other way around.

---

# Important distinction: Senior vs Staff

Do not artificially rewrite the portfolio to make me look “Staff” if the evidence does not support it.

Instead, identify where the case studies currently demonstrate:

### Senior-level evidence
- high autonomy,
- complex execution,
- strong cross-functional collaboration,
- ownership of important product/system areas,
- mentoring,
- strong technical/design judgment.

### Staff-leaning evidence
- multi-team influence,
- organizational direction,
- long-term strategy,
- changing how multiple teams operate,
- setting standards beyond one product,
- influencing leadership/roadmaps,
- resolving systemic problems.

If Staff-level evidence is missing, tell me clearly.

Then ask whether I have other examples that could demonstrate it.

# Cursor workflow

Follow this sequence.

## Phase 1 — Audit

1. Inspect the portfolio repository.
2. Identify all case-study pages.
3. Identify current positioning/headline/about copy.
4. Identify repeated generic claims.
5. Identify where the portfolio shows outputs but not impact.
6. Identify unsupported or ambiguous claims.
7. Do **not** rewrite yet.

Return:

- strengths,
- weaknesses,
- missing evidence,
- priority pages,
- questions for me.

---

## Phase 2 — Evidence gathering

For the highest-priority case study:

1. Extract all factual claims already present.
2. Build a list of unanswered impact questions.
3. Ask me 3–6 questions.
4. Wait for my answers.
5. Update the evidence list.
6. Repeat until enough evidence exists.

---

## Phase 3 — Rewrite proposal

Before editing code, propose:

- new page structure,
- key story,
- strongest evidence,
- sections to remove,
- sections to shorten,
- visuals to add,
- missing information still needed.

Wait for confirmation if the changes are substantial.

---

## Phase 4 — Implementation

Then update the portfolio.

Preserve the existing visual identity unless a layout change is needed to improve storytelling.

Do not redesign everything just because you can.

Prioritize:

1. story,
2. hierarchy,
3. evidence,
4. readability,
5. visuals,
6. polish.

# Final quality check

Before considering a case study complete, answer:

- Can I explain my personal contribution in 30 seconds?
- Can a hiring manager identify which decisions were mine?
- Does the page show outcomes, not just outputs?
- Does it distinguish my work from team work?
- Does it show constraints without sounding defensive?
- Is there evidence for every important claim?
- Does it demonstrate how I think?
- Does it show influence where influence genuinely existed?
- Does it avoid invented metrics?
- Does it explain why this work matters?
- Is the strongest information visible without reading every paragraph?

If the answer to any is “no”, flag it.
